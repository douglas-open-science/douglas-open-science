# Les licences ouvertes

__Open licenses facilitate reuse of research outputs by a broad public and enable open science practices.__

Intellectual property is a complex topic, and __this webpage will help you navigate the topic of open licenses__. However, this is a brief guide, and you may still have questions left. For further support, please refer to services referenced throughout the page and the [“Need help?"](training-resources-opportunities.mdx#open-licenses-help) section.


## What are open licenses?

To understand how open licenses work, a key principle is that __intellectual property turns information into property__, meaning that its owner can decide how it gets used. Copyright is a type of intellectual property that turns the way information is expressed and organized into property (e.g., the order of words in an article, the organization of colours and shapes to form a picture), as long as this expression is at least a little creative.

 __A “license” is a legal way of expressing “permission”__. Think of open licenses as a blanket permission by the owner saying others can use that property in specific ways. The use cases allowed, which depend widely on the license chosen, would otherwise be conditional on obtaining permission from owners on a case-by-case basis.

 __Open licenses do not replace or infringe copyright – they work on top of it__. They rely on copyright laws, expire when copyright expires, and can only be applied to copyrightable work – typically, original work as opposed to facts or ideas. In practice, open licenses act as a [middle ground between copyright protection and public domain](https://creativecommons.org/faq/#what-does-some-rights-reserved-mean).

 __Differently from copyright, which is automatically applied, open licenses need to be actively applied to a work__. The following sections explain how to choose and attribute open licenses, with emphasis on three types of research outputs: [scientific publications](publication-licenses.mdx), [datasets](dataset-licenses.mdx), and [software](software-licenses.mdx).

 ## A note on fair dealing
 Canada’s Copyright Act provides for the use, or "dealing" with, of copyright-protected work without permission, provided that the use is “fair”. This enables practices such as transformative copying (e.g. parody or creation of educational materials) or the re-use of extracts from copyrighted material (e.g. reviews or criticism, previewing thumbnail images in search engines). Fair dealing might, for example, include sharing a research article with students. 

In general, to determine whether use qualifies as fair dealing, [courts consider factors such as its purpose, extent, and effect](https://libraryguides.mcgill.ca/c.php?g=710276#s-lg-box-15912928). __It is important to understand that the application of open licenses neither hinder nor facilitate fair dealing, since these kinds of use do not require permission from copyright holders.__
