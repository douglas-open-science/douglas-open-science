# Open measures

__Open measures are open standardized assessments (e.g., self-report questionnaires or semi-structured interviews) of a particular construct (e.g., symptoms, cognition)__. These measures are non-commercial and, ideally, are shared under open licenses (upcoming) that explicitly allow reuse without seeking additional permission from the author. Examples of open measures are the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAM-A), in the public domain, and the Goal Based Outcomes (GBO) tool, available under a CC license.

The openness of measures is [on a spectrum](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5766425/): __measures in the public domain or under a [CC-BY license](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/) are on the opposite end of the spectrum from those commercialized by for-profit organizations, which are typically considered “closed”__; in between, there are measures with more restrictive licenses and/or that require seeking individual permission from copyright holders.

__In contrast to most commercial measures, open measures are free of charge__. Imagine the following scenario: for some measures, the total cost (e.g., manuals, record forms, reports, response booklets) to screen just 25 participants, excluding any other costs specific to your project, may be above $3,000! In the long-term, adopting open measures would substantially reduce costs for psychological assessments.

__Open measures provide accountability and allow verifying the quality of research and clinical assessments__. Researchers can better understand how a measure works, assess its construct validity (i.e. if it assesses what it aims to), and modify or revise the measure as needed, [conserving the long-term relevance of measures](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0350023). The ease of reusability and uptake can also translate to important gains in [generalizability](https://iamhrf.org/common-measures/).

In certain cases, such as self-report measures, __there is a concern that open measures may increase the rate of false diagnosis and misapplications__: participants being aware of the measures prior to assessment could lead to [exposure-related biases](https://doi.org/10.2196/68735) or strategic response bias. However, there are known strategies (e.g., [automatic item generation](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/bysa2_v1) or [modelling response bias](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0243384)) to mitigate such effects. Moreover, [closed measures may face similar risks](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S000169182500753X).

## Practical steps
* __Review the basics on identifying appropriate measures for behavioral assessment__, using the [McGill library guide](https://libraryguides.mcgill.ca/measures) on finding instruments, measures, scales and tests in education, health, psychology, and social work. We also recommend these [tutorials](https://libguides.usask.ca/psychology/tests) from the University of Saskatchewan’s guide on Psychology: Tests & Measures.
* __Include the open measure as part of your ethics submission before using it in your research__ and know that certain measures may only be administered by licensed or certified professionals.
* __Learn how to identify open measures, understanding that openness is on a spectrum__. Databases such as [APA PsycTests](https://www.apa.org/pubs/databases/psyctests) (consult their [reference guide](https://www.apa.org/pubs/databases/training/psycnet.pdf)) summarize reuse permissions and facilitate this task. Use the [McGill library guide](https://libraryguides.mcgill.ca/measures/known) to access these databases with McGill credentials. When assessing a measure's openness, follow these steps:
  * __Identify the exact name and version of the measure__ – reuse conditions may depend on versioning. Referencing persistent identifiers such as DOIs may aid in differentiating between different measure versions.
  * __Verify if the measure is publicly available online, free of charge__ – be aware that this does not ensure openness and that you may encounter copies shared without permission and against the rights of copyright holders. Open measures are often available through databases such as [APA PsycTests](https://www.apa.org/pubs/databases/psyctests), open repositories, or as part of the scientific publications first describing them. Some non-commercial measures may have restricted access, dependent on your credentials, or only be available upon request. In contrast, commercial measures often require payment of fees, although academic agreements can be possible at times.
  * __Understand what you can and cannot do with the measure__ (e.g., apply it, reproduce it in your work, or adapt it). Permissions may depend on the use case and user (e.g., researcher, clinician, or research trainees). The open license terms outline the permissions and restrictions to the reuse of the tool. If the measure lacks an open license or clearly outlined terms of use, contact the copyright holder for clarification.
* __When developing open measures__:
  * __Provide structured, clear manuals.__ This will help users follow best practices.
  * __Consider digital implementation of your open measures. This allows you to acquire data in digital formats that align with open science principles.__ Contact the [Douglas Neuroinformatics Platform Team](mailto:contact@douglasneuroinformatics.ca) to learn about currently available open measures in the [Open Data Capture Platform](https://opendatacapture.org/en/) and discuss possible new implementations.
  * __Attribute open licenses to your open measures and accompanying documentation.__ Consult guidance from our open license resource (coming soon!). 
  * __Assign the appropriate persistent identifiers to your open measures__, typically DOIs. Consult our [persistent identifiers resource][../Persistent_Identifiers] for more information.
