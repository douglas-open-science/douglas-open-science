# Open hardware

__Open hardware is one of the open science practices included in [UNESCO’s 2021 Recommendation on Open Science](https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000379949)__. As outlined by [Opensource.com](https://opensource.com/resources/what-open-hardware), open hardware includes __any physical hardware__ (e.g., equipment or tools used for scientific research) __whose design is made available to the public [under licenses](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/licenses.html), which are similar to those of open software and allow for reuse and adaptations to the greatest extent as possible__.

__Open hardware facilitates the design and manufacture of physical goods, reducing roadblocks created by restrictive intellectual property protection and elevated costs__. Strikingly, the materials needed to replicate open hardware may cost [only one tenth of the cost of a commercial device](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3001931), if it is available on the market at all. For example, the [Glia Stethoscope Project](https://glia.org/pages/stethoscope) uses 3D printing to [reduce manufacturing costs by 14-fold](https://glia.org/pages/stethoscope)!

__This open science practice promotes collaboration by allowing researchers to replicate or build upon designs developed by others__. For example, 3D printing designs are often shared in the open hardware community and contain information to produce either prototypes of the desired hardware or the open hardware itself. An example of the latter is [3D designs that can be printed to produce animal holders for MRI applications](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165027018303832) or [MRI scanners themselves](https://www.opensourceimaging.org/2023/01/09/first-open-source-mri-scanner-presented-the-osii-one/).

:::guide[Open hardware at the Douglas]
At the Douglas, the [M3 platform](https://m3platform.weebly.com/) uses the [UCLA miniscope](http://miniscope.org/index.php/Main_Page), an open-source miniature fluorescence camera and microscope that can be mounted on the mouse skull. Using a variation of the miniscope, they monitor and record calcium levels in multiple brain areas during diverse types of tests.
:::

## Practical steps
* __Identify initiatives for open hardware and open innovation in mental health research__, such as [Open Ephys](https://open-ephys.org/) and [3D neuro](https://www.3dneuro.com/) for electrophysiology applications or [Open Neuroscience](https://open-neuroscience.com/en/) for a general database. 
* __Join open hardware communities__, such as [Gathering for Open Science Hardware](https://openhardware.science/) and [Open Hardware Makers](https://openhardware.space/).
* Learn best practices for reusing and sharing open hardware – consult [Open Hardware Makers](https://openhardware.space/) and the [Open Source Hardware Association (OSHWA)](https://oshwa.org/resources/sharing-best-practices/) (including their [FAQ page](https://oshwa.org/resources/open-source-hardware-faq/)) for guidelines (see resources), community, and expert support. 
  * When __reusing open hardware__, respect the copyright and license terms of others. If improving the design, share your changes with the original creator, and release the new version as open hardware if possible.
  * When __sharing open hardware__, identity the purpose of the design in plain language. Share all resources needed to reproduce it, such as code required to produce or utilize the hardware, a list of physical materials used to build it, photos describing the building process, and clear usage instructions. If possible, design your projects using [open software](./software).
* __Build or 3D print open hardware using installations available to our community__:
  * Use 3D printers available at McGill University (e.g., at the [Redpath Library](https://libraryrooms.mcgill.ca/reserve/3d-printers)). Learn more through the [McGill Library guide on 3D printing](https://libraryguides.mcgill.ca/3d) and find out about other resources offered by McGill such as [microcontrollers](https://libraryguides.mcgill.ca/microcontrollers) and the [Nanotools-MicroFab laboratory](https://www.mcgill.ca/miam/nanotools).
  * Contact us to discuss 3D printers available at the Douglas.
* __Find and share open hardware through repositories__ such as [DocuBricks](http://docubricks.com/), which supports standardised open hardware documentation, [GitHub](https://github.com/) and [hackaday.io](https://hackaday.io/?from=had&utm_source=hackaday&utm_medium=menu&utm_campaign=blog). The Journal of Open Hardware compares repositories [here](https://ojs.lib.uwo.ca/index.php/openhardware/about). For animal behavior studies, check for example the [OpenBehavior](https://edspace.american.edu/openbehavior/) project.
* __Attribute open licenses to open hardware and accompanying documentation, design files, and software__. Consult guidance from our open license webpage (coming soon!) and the Open-Source Hardware Association [licensing guidelines](https://certification.oshwa.org/process/hardware.html). 
* __Assign persistent identifiers to open hardware and accompanying files__. RRIDs are commonly used to identify research equipment. Consult our [persistent identifiers resource](../Persistent_Identifiers/other-applications#pids-for-research-resources) for additional guidance.
